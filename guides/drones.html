Keelhaul

paper lineage

Needle Dart and Nakamura Lock, borrowed for exactly one principle: concentrate the densest mass forward so component placement (not dead ballast) sets the CG. Explicitly NOT borrowed: their tiny low-aspect swept-delta wings and brute wing loading — those are low-Re artefacts that would hurt a real drone. NOTE: unlike those tailless darts, Keelhaul is a TAILED aircraft, so its stability is governed by the aircraft neutral point (wing + V-tail together), not by the wing aerodynamic centre alone.

reynolds reality

The transferable half — forward mass placement to set the CG, and CG-ahead-of-NP for a positive static margin — is pure rigid-body statics and is completely Reynolds-independent; it works identically at paper-plane Re ~30k and at this drone's cruise Re ~192k (V=16 m/s, MAC=0.18 m: 16*0.18/1.5e-5 = 192,000). Correction to the original framing: static margin is (NP - CG)/MAC for this TAILED layout, not (wing-AC - CG); the wing AC is only the neutral point for a tailless wing, which this is not. The NON-transferable half is everything else the paper darts rely on: their sharp swept-delta leading edges and stubby AR~1 wings tolerate separation only because at Re~30k the boundary layer is forgiving and glide performance barely matters. At Re~2e5 that same low-AR, high-wing-loading dart is a draggy, fast, short-endurance brick. So Keelhaul keeps the forward-mass idea but flips the wing: AR~5.8, moderate wing loading (~50 g/dm2), a cambered SD7037-class section that behaves well at 1e5-3e5. Verdict partially-scales: the mass/CG principle scales fully; the paper aerodynamics do not.

efficiency gain

Two related benefits from one decision (correct CG via component placement); they are not fully independent, so do not simply add them. (1) Mass: using the dense LiPo as forward mass avoids the dead nose ballast that a badly-planned tail-heavy build would need — this is good practice rather than a novel efficiency, so treat it as 'no wasted grams' not a guaranteed 5-8% endurance win. (2) Trim drag: a correct static margin lets the aircraft cruise near zero elevator/ruddervator deflection, so the tail carries almost no trim load. Against a poorly-trimmed baseline this is worth on the order of a few percent of cruise power (optimistically up to ~3-6%); at the low cruise CL~0.31 the absolute trim drag is small, so this is a modest, not dramatic, gain. Net: a clean, stable, hands-off cruise for zero added ballast hardware — a sensible design, not a breakthrough.

weighted components / CG

The layout is organised so the CG sits at/ahead of the wing aerodynamic centre AND comfortably ahead of the aircraft neutral point. From the diagram the wing root chord runs y=38 (LE) to y=52 (TE), so the wing AC (0.25c) is at y=41.5, NOT y~47 as originally misstated. The aircraft NP — wing plus the aft V-tail on the ~0.5 m boom — sits further aft, estimated around y~50-52 (tail volume ~0.4). Target CG is y~41 (right at the wing AC), which places it well ahead of the NP and yields a healthy static margin on the order of ~10-20% MAC once the tail is properly accounted for. To land the CG there without dead lead, the LiPo (heavy) is the primary forward mass in the nose bay at y~12, while the pusher motor is kept aft at y~93 as a deliberate counterweight that lengthens the moment arm, so a modest fore/aft battery slide in the PETG cradle tunes the CG (and hence static margin) precisely. Flight controller and GPS (medium/light) sit just behind the battery (y~26, y~34). Every forward gram does structural, electrical AND balance duty. The deep keel-like pod ahead of the CG contributes a little fixed side area; note it is FORWARD of the CG so it is slightly DESTABILIZING in yaw — the V-tail's vertical projection aft of the CG is what actually provides yaw stability and damping.

how it prints

Fuselage pod printed in LW-PLA (foaming PLA, ~0.4-0.5 g/cm3 effective) with 2-perimeter walls and 8-10% gyroid infill for a light stiff shell. Nose bay is the exception: a solid-walled PETG cradle (3 perimeters, no foaming) that the LiPo slides into on a friction fit against a printed hard bulkhead at the tip — PETG for impact toughness and heat tolerance, its density used as useful forward mass. A single 8 mm pultruded carbon tube is the spanwise main spar, bonded into LW-PLA ribs/skin (solid-ish printed D-box leading edge, sparse trailing edge). A 6 mm carbon boom carries the pusher motor and V-tail aft. Control surfaces (2 ruddervators; elevon-style mixing, no separate ailerons) use INTEGRAL thin-web print-in-place hinges printed flat with the surface. IMPORTANT CORRECTION: a TPU living hinge cannot be co-printed inside a PLA/PETG surface on a single nozzle — so either (a) print the hinge as a ~0.4 mm integral PLA/PETG web (simple, single-nozzle, but fatigues and eventually needs reprint), or (b) use a genuine multi-material printer (2 extruders or filament-switching) to lay TPU webs into the PETG surfaces for durable flex hinges. Option (b) is assumed here and is why build difficulty is 'hard'. Servos sit in PETG pockets; wing halves join over the carbon spar with a PETG saddle + M3 nylon bolts for field disassembly.

tradeoffs

Forward CG plus aft pusher motor make the plane nose-heavy on the ground and demand a firm hand-launch or bungee at Vstall ~8.6 m/s, not a lazy toss. The deep keel pod adds wetted area/parasitic drag and, being forward of the CG, is mildly yaw-destabilizing — the V-tail must be sized to win that argument. LW-PLA is stiff-per-gram but brittle in a hard nose-in; the PETG cradle protects the battery but the LW-PLA wing skin will crack in a bad crash (cheap to reprint). Multi-material TPU hinges need a capable printer; the single-nozzle PLA-web fallback fatigues. Sliding-battery CG tuning is a genuine advantage but demands the builder actually balance it: get it tail-heavy (CG behind the NP) and the static margin goes negative and pitch becomes divergent. Realistic AUW is 950-1050 g depending on print settings, not a guaranteed 950 g. Print time ~18-24 h across parts.

span 1.8 mAR AR 12 (S=0.27 m^2, mean chord 150 mm)AUW ~1.8 kg all-up (structure ~650 g, spars ~180 g, battery ~500 g / ~90 Wh, avionics+payload+motors ~470 g)

paper lineage

Sea Glider / Edmond Hui Paperang high-aspect cruise wing. Borrowed principle: glide ratio equals L/D, and at cruise the dominant loss is induced drag, whose coefficient falls in proportion to aspect ratio at fixed wing area — so a long, thin, low-wing-loading wing trims to a slow, flat, efficient glide. Also borrows the Sea Glider's rolled leading-edge hem idea, reinterpreted as a carbon spar bonded at the quarter-chord that both stiffens the LE and helps pull CG forward toward the wing's quarter-chord.

reynolds reality

This is the honest case where the paper trick DOES transfer, because the underlying lever — induced drag falling with aspect ratio — is Reynolds-independent inviscid geometry, not a viscous low-Re effect. At mean chord 150 mm and cruise 13.3 m/s, chord Reynolds is ~133,000 — comfortably ABOVE the paper regime (~30-50k), which helps: flow stays attached more readily, so we do NOT inherit the Sea Glider's fussy laminar-separation behaviour. What does NOT transfer is the paper plane's razor-sharp folded leading edge (fine at Re~30k, but at Re>100k a small nose radius and a real low-camber airfoil, e.g. a 9% Selig/Clark-Y-ish profile, give lower drag and gentler stall). Net: the aspect-ratio payoff is real and even cleaner at drone scale; we use a proper printed airfoil instead of a folded crease. Caveat: the AR benefit acts only on INDUCED drag — it does nothing for the parasite drag of four exposed lift motors, booms and clamps, which dominates this airframe's real CD0.

efficiency gain

Going from a typical AR~5 VTOL wing to AR~12 at the same 0.27 m^2 area more than halves the induced-drag coefficient at cruise CL (CDi ~0.011 vs ~0.027 at CL0.6, verified), which is the real, Reynolds-independent win. With a CLEAN airframe (CD0~0.03) that lifts cruise L/D from ~9-10 to ~14.6 (best L/D ~16). BUT this quadplane carries four exposed lift motors, booms and clamps in the freestream; a realistic installed CD0 is ~0.04-0.05, so honest cruise L/D is ~11-14 and cruise electrical power ~30-34 W (not 30 W as a clean floor). The physics is right — L/D_max = 0.5*sqrt(pi*e*AR/CD0) grows as sqrt(AR) — but parasite drag caps the payoff. On a REAL 90 Wh / 500 g pack (the 178 Wh in 500 g of the original is impossible at 356 Wh/kg), usable cruise range is ~110-140 km, call it ~120 km nominal — still a strong ~40-50% range gain over an AR~5 equivalent, just not the 200-230 km originally claimed.

weighted components / CG

CG must sit AHEAD of the aircraft NEUTRAL POINT (NP) for positive static margin. Two references matter and were conflated in the original: (a) the WING aerodynamic centre at the wing quarter-chord (y=41.5 in the box), and (b) the whole-aircraft NP, which sits AFT of the wing AC because the tail/ruddervator adds lift-curve slope at a moment arm. CG is placed at y=39.8 — about 12% MAC ahead of the wing AC, and therefore comfortably ahead of the NP by MORE than that (a healthy ~10-18% static margin against the true NP; safely stable). We load heavy items forward: the battery (heaviest single item, ~500 g 6S LiPo, ~90 Wh) rides in the nose tray well forward of AC and is the primary CG-trim tool — slide it fore/aft on rails to null pitch trim after any payload change. The camera/gimbal payload and GPS sit just behind the nose, still ahead of AC. The flight controller sits near CG so IMU rotations stay clean. The rear pusher motor and ESC sit at the tail (y~92), their moment balancing the nose mass so the battery need not be extreme-forward. The FOUR lift rotors are on outrigger booms straddling the wing; the front pair is at y=33 and the rear pair moved forward to y=53 so the rotor-pair midpoint (y=43) lands close to CG (y=39.8), keeping hover pitch nearly decoupled from the CG offset (the original y=57 rear pair put the midpoint at y=45, biased aft of CG). Because a 1.8 m wing has ~16x the roll inertia of a compact 0.45 m quad of equal mass (Ixx ~0.24 vs 0.015 kg m^2), roll authority in hover is the hard problem: we push the lift booms as far OUTBOARD as structure allows (rotor track ~0.9 m, arm ~0.45 m) and size each rotor at T/W~1.6 for ~270 g per-rotor thrust headroom. Rolling the two windward rotors up and the two leeward rotors down by that headroom yields ~4.8 Nm and ~1100 deg/s^2 in hover — actually MORE than adequate on paper, but rate is inertia-limited and roll will still FEEL slow versus a racing quad because the aircraft accelerates then must be arrested against the same large inertia. Yaw uses rotor-torque differential; pitch uses fore/aft boom-pair differential.

how it prints

Wing skin printed in LW-PLA (foaming lightweight PLA) at 0.4 mm single-perimeter walls, in ~250 mm spanwise segments keyed together over a full-length pultruded carbon spar. Spar: 10 mm OD x 8 mm ID carbon tube running the full 1.8 m at the wing quarter-chord (the primary bending member — LW-PLA alone cannot carry the ~1 g bending of a 1.8 m span). A secondary 6 mm carbon tube at ~60% chord resists torsion. Fuselage pod and the two lift-rotor booms: PETG for toughness and UV/heat tolerance where motors dump heat. Aileron and ruddervator hinges: print-in-place TPU living hinges (95A), a 0.6 mm TPU web co-printed as the trailing-edge skin so there are no metal pins to fatigue. Rotor booms clamp to the spar with PETG saddle clamps + M3 bolts so the outboard track is adjustable. Motor mounts: PETG with brass heat-set inserts. Nose cone and battery tray: PETG (impact + payload security). Estimated printed structure ~650 g, spars ~180 g. This structural mass budget is realistic for a 1.8 m span; the fantasy in the original was the battery, not the print.

tradeoffs

The long span pays for its efficiency four ways. (1) Hover roll control is degraded ~16x by span-squared roll inertia; differential thrust gives adequate moment (~4.8 Nm) but rate is inertia-limited, so gusty low-altitude hover is the weakest flight phase. (2) Structural: a 1.8 m LW-PLA wing needs a real carbon spar and torsion tube or it will flutter/diverge; the segmented print introduces keyed, bonded joints that must be built carefully. (3) Ground/transport handling and crosswind sensitivity increase with span. (4) Parasite drag from the four exposed lift rotors and booms is significant and does NOT benefit from the high aspect ratio, capping real cruise L/D at ~11-14. Hover is power-hungry (~195-200 W elec vs ~30-34 W cruise), so on a real 90 Wh pack only ~4-5 min of hover is budgeted — the profile must be hover-briefly / transition-fast / cruise-long. This is a cruise-optimised airframe that merely tolerates hover, not a loiter-in-place multirotor. If instead you want the original 178 Wh energy, the pack weighs ~1.05 kg, AUW rises to ~2.35 kg, hover T/W falls to ~1.2 (below the 1.6 target), and stall speed rises — a genuine and unattractive trade.

paper lineage

Ring-Wing Glider (annular/closed-wing principle) plus the Bulldog Dart's forward-CG blunt-nose mass stacking. From the Ring-Wing it borrows exactly ONE transferable idea: a closed (annular/box) lifting surface has no free wingtips, so it does not shed the usual pair of strong trailing tip vortices. From the Bulldog it borrows the multi-layer forward mass concentration that pins the CG ahead of the aerodynamic centre.

reynolds reality

HONEST verdict: the SPECIFIC paper ring-wing benefit does NOT scale. The paper hoop (chord ~10-15mm, Re ~2-4x10^4) works because at that Re a curved surface avoids the leading-edge separation that stalls a flat paper wing, and its "no tip vortex" claim is really about a tiny, low-circulation loop. At this drone's cruise (chord ~90mm, 16 m/s, Re ~96,000; duct-lip Re ~1.3x10^5) flow stays attached on a proper cambered section anyway, so the curved-ring anti-separation trick buys nothing. What DOES survive is the geometric truth underneath it: a genuinely CLOSED lifting system has no tip vortices, and Prandtl's box-wing induced-drag reduction (ideal ~1.47x span efficiency at h/b=0.2, ~1.3x realized) is a potential-flow result independent of Re. Likewise duct thrust augmentation is momentum-theory-based and if anything works BETTER at higher Re (thinner boundary layers, less lip separation). So we keep the closed-wing geometry and the ducts, and discard the "ring beats a flat wing" reasoning. Net: the shape transfers, the paper-scale justification does not.

efficiency gain

Two stacked, physically-real but MODEST gains. (1) Cruise: the closed box-wing cuts induced drag toward ~0.68 of an equal-span monoplane in the ideal limit (span efficiency up to ~1.47 ideal, ~1.3 realized). Because this is a draggy airframe (three ducts, box structure, fuselage give CD0~0.05) and cruise CL is only ~0.5, induced drag is NOT the dominant term, so the realized cruise L/D improvement over an open monoplane of equal span is roughly 5-14%, NOT 20-30%. (2) Hover/transition: ducting the two lift rotors augments static thrust ~1.2-1.5x for the same disk power and recovers blade-tip leakage, so hover is meaningfully more efficient for a given footprint. Note the disk loading is high (~310 N/m^2 with two 6-inch rotors), so hover power is not cheap even with the duct benefit. Prop safety (fully shrouded rotors) is a free bonus of the same geometry.

weighted components / CG

CG/static-margin logic: the aerodynamic centre (neutral point) of a box-wing sits near the mid-chord of the wing system, which here is around y=55 in the top-view box (mean between the front element at y~42 and rear element at y~68). For static pitch stability the CG must sit AHEAD (lower y) of that AC with a positive static margin of ~10-15% of the mean aerodynamic chord. I place the two heaviest items — the LiPo battery (heavy, y=30) and the forward flight-controller/GPS stack — well forward in the nose pod, and add a small dense tungsten ballast tray at the very nose (y=22) exactly as the Bulldog Dart stacks paper layers forward. The two lift-duct motors are mounted ON the fore and aft duct centerlines (both at x=50, y=42 and y=68) so their combined thrust line passes through the centerline and near the CG, keeping hover trim neutral in both roll and pitch — earlier off-center placement would have produced a rolling moment in hover. The rear pusher motor (medium, y=88) is aft but light and its moment is deliberately balanced by the nose ballast. Result: CG lands at y≈47, roughly 10% MAC ahead of the AC at y≈55 — a stable positive static margin without being so nose-heavy it needs excessive cruise up-trim.

how it prints

Wing box + fuselage pod printed in LW-PLA (foaming, ~0.5-0.6 g/cc effective) at 2-perimeter, 5% gyroid infill for a light, stiff aero shell. The two annular lift ducts and the rear cruise duct are printed as thin (1.2mm wall) LW-PLA shrouds with an integral bellmouth lip. Because the front and rear wing elements are separated both fore-aft and by the ~164mm vertical box gap, they cannot share a single straight spar: each element is stiffened by its OWN 6mm pultruded carbon spar (two spars total), and the duct thrust and bending loads react into whichever element's spar they sit nearest. Fore and aft wing elements are joined at the tips by printed PETG end-plates (the closing members of the box) — PETG for its higher toughness where the closed loop takes the most bending and impact. Elevon and rudder hinges are printed as thin (0.4mm) TPU living hinges co-printed into the trailing edges (print-in-place, no pins). Motor mounts and the nose ballast tray are PETG for stiffness and heat tolerance near the ESCs. Bolted joints use heat-set brass inserts in PETG bosses. Total part count kept low: ~6 printed pieces plus 2 carbon spars. Weight realism: a 360g LW-PLA airframe is optimistic for this much wetted area (box wing + three ducts + end-plates); ~400-480g is more likely, so plan for AUW creeping toward ~1200-1300g.

tradeoffs

The closed box-wing is heavier and far more complex to build than a simple wing — two spanwise spars (one per element), tip end-plates, and three printed ducts add structural mass and drag-producing wetted area, so much of the ideal induced-drag win is eaten back by extra profile/interference drag; the net L/D gain is real but only ~5-14%, well short of the ideal 1.47x. Ducts add mass and wetted-area drag in fast forward flight (the lift ducts become dead weight and blockage once transitioned), and duct-lip separation in edgewise/gusty flight can bite. The small 6-inch lift disks give a high disk loading (~310 N/m^2), so hover is power-hungry and gust-sensitive. The h/b=0.2 gap that maximises the box-wing benefit also makes the airframe ~164mm tall and less compact to transport. Build difficulty and part fit-up (getting both spars, both lift ducts, and the tip end-plates concentric and square) are the main practical risks.

paper lineage

The Harrier (Aerobatic Loop Glider). Borrowed principle: tailless pitch trim and control via reflex + trailing-edge elevons, with pitch stability bought entirely by a deliberately forward CG rather than a horizontal stabilizer. The Harrier's 'up-elevon reflex + forward CG = stable without a tail' logic is the whole airframe philosophy here; we drop the loop-mission (elevons deflect down/reflex-neutral for efficient cruise instead of up for looping). Note: only the geometric/stability logic is borrowed - the paper's flat-plate airfoil is explicitly discarded (see Reynolds note).

reynolds reality

The Harrier as paper flies at Re~30k-50k where a flat, low-camber plate with a sharp leading edge works fine because flow is barely attached anyway. This drone cruises at ~16 m/s, chord ~0.169 m, giving Re~183,000 (range ~165k-200k across the CL band) - 4-6x higher. At that Re a sharp-edged flat plate is actively bad: you want a real cambered/reflexed airfoil with a rounded leading edge (an MH-class reflexed section, e.g. MH60/MH45), which holds attached flow and gives an L/D of ~12-14 vs the paper plate's ~4-5. So the specific paper AIRFOIL does NOT scale and must be replaced. HOWEVER, the borrowed design PRINCIPLE - tailless layout, trailing-edge reflex for nose-up Cm0, elevon trim/control, and forward-CG-for-stability - is Reynolds-independent and transfers cleanly, as the concept itself concedes. That mixed result is why the honest verdict is 'partially-scales', not 'does-not-scale': the stability geometry scales, the flat-plate section does not.

efficiency gain

Removing the tailplane and tail boom cuts roughly 15-20% of total WETTED area and eliminates the tail's structural mass and download. Against a well-designed TAILED RC wing of the same wing area (which would also use a proper airfoil), the realistic cruise-power saving from going tailless is ~10-15%, not 20-30% - because induced drag (CDi~0.014 at cruise CL~0.49) is the dominant component and is unchanged by tail removal, and the tail is only a fraction of total parasite drag. The larger, separate win - swapping the paper's flat plate (L/D ~4-5) for a reflexed section (L/D ~12-14) - is a gain over the PAPER, not over a modern tailed design, so it must not be stacked on top of the tailless saving. Cruise aero power is ~16-19W (drag ~1.0-1.2N at 16 m/s), ~30-45W at the shaft. The genuine advantage remains real: the whole airframe is lifting surface with no non-lifting boom or stabilizer.

weighted components / CG

Everything heavy is pushed FORWARD of the wing neutral point to buy static margin a tailless wing has no tail to provide. tradeoffs

Real, not glossed. paper lineage

Nakamura Lock: the exact principle borrowed is its signature 'locked nose' — stacking and pinning several layers at the front so mass concentrates ahead of the wing's aerodynamic center, producing a positive static-stability margin that self-corrects rather than pitches or dives. I translate the paper 'lock' (folded-and-pinned layers that never crumple, keeping CG and airfoil intact throw after throw) into a 3D-print methodology: snap-fit self-locking joints and a solid, non-deforming forward battery bay that holds CG constant flight after flight. The broad, blunt, low-wing-loading planform and shallow dihedral Y are carried over directly. What is NOT carried over is the paper's disregard for the exact size of the margin: a paper dart tolerates an enormous forward CG, a powered cruiser does not, so the borrowed idea is bounded to a controllable ~8-10% MAC.

reynolds reality

The paper Nakamura flies at Re ~20,000-40,000. This drone cruises at Re ~170,000 (V ~11.3 m/s, chord 0.22 m), roughly 4-8x higher. What DOES scale: the CG/static-margin logic is Reynolds-independent — forward mass gives pitch stability at any Re, so the 'locked forward CG = self-correcting slow cruiser' behavior transfers directly, and this is the design's backbone. What does NOT scale: the paper's tolerance for a blunt, thick, slightly-crumpled low-camber section relies on low-Re flow reattaching easily; at Re ~170k a blunt or rough leading edge instead trips early separation and costs L/D. So I keep the blunt planform (for CG and slow-flight margin) but replace the 'any old fold' airfoil with a defined ~9% low-camber section and a clean, sanded leading edge. Net: the stability trick fully transfers; the aerodynamic sloppiness does not and must be engineered out. Honest caveat: at Re ~170k even a clean version of this thick, low-AR planform is a modest-L/D wing, not a sailplane.

efficiency gain

Two gains, honestly bounded. (1) Structural (the real one): tool-free snap-fit + TPU living hinges + gyroid LW-PLA cut fastener/bearing count to near zero and drop empty-airframe mass roughly 20-30% vs a screwed PETG-solid equivalent, lowering wing loading (~4.8 kg/m2, ~48 g/dm2) and thus sink rate — a genuine energy benefit. (2) Aerodynamic/operational: AR ~4.1 with a clean engineered section gives a realistic cruise L/D around 7-9 (drag buildup: induced at e~0.75 plus section and parasite drag from the pod, three nacelles, exposed spar and snap seams). The fixed, known CG mainly buys low pilot workload and good gust rejection; any cruise-power benefit from reduced trim corrections is small (~1-3%), and note a forward CG slightly INCREASES up-elevator trim drag. Correcting the earlier claim: static stability is NOT free cruise energy — the honest saving is structural mass, not a 10-20% current reduction.

weighted components / CG

The rule: heavy items go FORWARD, in a rigid bay, to pin the CG a MODEST ~8-10% MAC ahead of the neutral point — not as far forward as a paper dart. On a tailless layout the NP sits ~at the wing AC (~y48 in this planform, nudged slightly forward by the fuselage), so the target CG is ~y47, only about one chord-tenth ahead of it. The 4S LiPo (heaviest single item, ~200 g) sits in the forward battery bay at y~30; the flight controller + GPS ride behind it (y~40-45). The wing AC/neutral point is at ~y48. The resulting CG at (50, 47) sits just ahead of the NP — a controllable positive static margin of ~8-10% MAC (chord ~11 units, so ~1 unit of margin). That small forward offset IS the static stability: any nose-up disturbance raises AoA, lift at the AC produces a nose-down restoring moment about the slightly-forward CG, so the aircraft self-corrects — the Nakamura behavior, bounded to a flyable margin. The battery is the CG trim knob: sliding it fore/aft in its rails tunes margin without redesign. The rear rotor (medium, aft at y~88) TILTS to lift in hover, giving the third pitch-control point; in hover the three rotors straddle the CG (two ahead at y~30, one behind at y~88) so pitch is fully controllable. In cruise the same rotor is a pusher.

how it prints

Wing skins/ribs in LW-PLA (foaming, ~0.6-0.8 g/cm3) printed thin (0.4-0.6 mm walls) with a sparse gyroid infill (12-15%) for stiffness-to-weight; a pultruded carbon spar (6 mm round or 8x0.8 mm tube) slides through print-in-place D-box rib holes as the main bending member. Fuselage pod in PETG for toughness and heat tolerance near ESCs. All THREE motor nacelles tilt on print-in-place TPU (95A) living hinges printed monolithically with the nacelle — hover-to-cruise rotation driven by servo pushrods (front pair on a common linkage, rear on its own servo); TPU tolerates thousands of flex cycles without a pinned bearing. Assembly is entirely snap-fit: wing panels engage the fuselage via dovetail + cantilever-snap latches (PETG, ~0.5 mm interference) that 'lock' like the Nakamura nose — no screws, no glue. Layered/gyroid stiffening is concentrated at the spar box and the snap tongues. All hinge and snap features print flat-on-bed for layer-shear strength across the load path.

tradeoffs

A modest forward CG (~8-10% SM) costs a little trim drag and makes the craft deliberately not agile — it resists maneuvering, the price of a stable cruiser (the paper Nakamura's trade vs a dart, but bounded here to stay controllable rather than nose-diving). LW-PLA is fragile and heat-sensitive: it cannot sit near ESCs (hence PETG pod) and dents on hard landings. TPU living hinges are the fatigue-life risk — now on all THREE rotors — field-replaceable, but a torn hinge grounds that rotor and, for the rear one, hover pitch control. Making the rear rotor tilt (required for hover) adds a third tilt servo and mixing complexity vs a plain plank wing or a fixed pusher. Snap-fit joints loosen with cycling and can rattle; interference must be tuned per printer. Blunt low-AR planform limits top-end L/D vs a true high-aspect sailplane. Weight budget is tight: the tri-tilt hardware eats margin, so 0.95 kg AUW is optimistic-realistic and demands disciplined LW-PLA printing.

paper lineage

Core planform philosophy is lifted from The Needle Dart: accept a small, low-aspect-ratio, high-wing-loading wing and make up the missing lift by other means. The Needle Dart does it with raw throw speed (high dynamic pressure from the arm); Bellwether does it with blown dynamic pressure from prop-wash. The exact transferable principle is 'lift = q x S x CL, so if you can raise local q you can shrink S' — the dart raises q with velocity, the drone raises q with the slipstream, though only usefully in the slow transition corridor where the wing is near stall. Forward-CG static-margin logic (heavy layers stacked ahead of the aerodynamic centre) is borrowed from the Nakamura Lock / Bulldog Dart nose-stack idea, realised here as a dense battery-and-payload nose mass — but tempered to a sane ~8% MAC margin rather than the extreme forward ballast a paper dart tolerates.

reynolds reality

Split the mechanism in two. (a) The pure dynamic-pressure / powered-lift q-boost is Reynolds-agnostic and DOES survive to drone scale — this is the part that lowers transition/stall speed and it works at any Re. (b) The much-touted low-Re bonus — turbulent slipstream killing the laminar-separation bubble for CLmax gains 'up to ~57%' in UIUC/Ananda-Deters-Selig rig data — is a LOW-Re artifact by the concept's own evidence: those gains peak at Re 30,000-80,000 precisely because that is where the bubble cripples a clean wing. The clean wing here cruises at Re ~200,000 where the bubble is already weak, so mechanism (b) contributes little in cruise; it only helps across the transition Re band (~50k-120k) during slow flight. NASA X-57 confirms powered lift is not a small-scale artifact (measured CL 3.45 -> 6.43, ratio 1.86), but that is a high-lift-flap-plus-blow CLmax ratio at low speed, NOT a cruise q-multiplier. Honest working number: assume an effective blown-region q-ratio of ~1.6-1.7 over ~55% of span, and expect the bubble-suppression bonus only in the transition corridor, not in cruise. Verdict: partially-scales.

efficiency gain

Honest, correctly-signed gains. (1) Wing shrink: with the blown region carrying ~1.6-1.7x dynamic pressure over ~55% of span, effective lift-area multiplier ~1.36, so the wing can be ~24% smaller in area (AR ~6 vs a ~9 baseline) for the same safe transition/stall speed. This buys reduced WING MASS and a stiffer, easier-to-print wing — worth real grams and structural robustness. (2) Free high-lift: unlike X-57's 12 dead-weight high-lift props switched off in cruise, Bellwether's blowers ARE its lift rotors, so zero propulsion-mass penalty for the blown capability and an inherently soft transition. Rough magnitude: ~10-15% lower transition power margin and a lighter/stiffer wing. IMPORTANT CORRECTION: the smaller wing does NOT reliably cut cruise drag. Dropping AR 9->6 raises induced drag at cruise CL ~0.43 enough to roughly cancel the parasite saving — a drag audit gives total wing drag ~0.69 N (AR9) vs ~0.72 N (AR6), i.e. essentially a wash, possibly slightly worse. So the earlier '5-8% cruise-drag reduction' claim is withdrawn; cruise drag is approximately neutral. And because cruise CL ~0.43 is far below stall, blowing is a TRANSITION tool, not a cruise-efficiency lever. Physics: raising local q lets the wing operate below stall through transition without large area — that is the whole payoff.

weighted components / CG

Heavy mass is stacked forward to buy a SANE static margin against a small low-AR wing whose aerodynamic centre (AC) sits at ~25% MAC. In the top-view box the wing occupies y~40 (leading edge) to y~47 (trailing edge), so MAC spans ~7 box units and the quarter-chord AC lands near y=41.9. The target CG is placed just AHEAD of it at y=41.3, giving a gap of ~0.6 box units = ~+8% MAC — genuinely stable but trimmable (the earlier y=38 target implied ~57% MAC, an untrimmable over-margin, and was an error). The dense 4S/6S Li-ion/LiPo battery is the CG-setting lever, sliding fore/aft on the tray to trim CG onto y=41.3 after build. Flight controller, GPS, and FPV/mapping payload sit in the nose (low y) to reinforce forward mass. CRITICALLY, the four tilt-rotor motors are re-spaced so the hover THRUST CENTROID coincides with the CG: front pair at y=36 (ahead of CG) and rear pair at y=47 (behind CG), centroid y=41.5 ~ CG — the earlier layout (front y=33, rear y=55, centroid y=44) put the thrust centroid ~6 units aft of the CG and would have pitched the craft nose-down in hover. Servos (tilt + flaperon) are light and near the roots so they barely move CG. Net: CG at (50, 41.3), AC at (50, 41.9), thrust centroid at (50, 41.5) — CG ahead of AC (statically pitch-stable) and on the thrust centroid (clean hover).

how it prints

Wing: LW-PLA (foaming PLA, realistically ~0.6-0.7 g/cm^3 when actively foamed) printed as thin-wall single-perimeter aerofoil shells in ~250 mm spanwise segments, threaded onto a 10 mm pultruded carbon tube main spar plus a 6 mm carbon rear spar for torsional stiffness (blown wings see real torsion from offset rotor thrust). Leading edge printed at 2 perimeters because prop-wash and grit hit there. Independent buildup: skin ~120 g, main spar ~75 g, rear spar ~28 g, ribs+LE ~50 g => wing+spar ~270 g (budget it at 0.27 kg, not 0.25). Wing-root ribs and tilt-rotor nacelle/pivot bosses in PETG for heat and fatigue resistance. Tilt mechanism: PETG nacelle yoke on a carbon-rod pivot, driven by a metal-gear servo through a printed PETG horn; TPU (95A) bushings damp motor vibration. TPU landing feet and living-hinge reflex flaperons (thin flexure, no metal hinge). Fuselage pod: PETG clamshell with an internal LW-PLA equipment tray. Motor mounts and spar-to-fuselage saddle: PETG with brass heat-set inserts. Bonded with CA + activator; spar removable for transport. All 3D-printable with commodity FDM; nothing exotic.

tradeoffs

Honest downsides. (1) In pure hover the wing is dead weight and a download surface under the rotors — blowing helps transition, not hover, so hover efficiency is slightly WORSE than a wingless quad of equal weight. (2) Tilt-rotors add mechanical complexity, failure modes, and vibration paths; the TPU-damped PETG nacelles are a genuine fatigue-risk area. (3) Asymmetric blow (one motor out) produces a strong coupled rolling+pitching moment because half the blown lift vanishes — the FC needs a blown-wing-specific failure mode, not just standard quad motor-out. (4) The blown wing makes a large nose-up pitching moment in the transition corridor that eats trim authority; the ~8% forward CG and reflex flaperons are sized for it but constrain how far aft CG can go. (5) Swirl and non-uniform blow keep the real q-ratio (~1.6-1.7) well below ideal (V_slip/V_free)^2 ~ 1.86 — do not oversell the shrink. (6) Low AR (~6) has higher induced drag than a high-AR sailplane wing, so this trades GLIDE/cruise efficiency for a compact, blowable, printable, transition-friendly planform — cruise drag is roughly neutral vs a larger high-AR wing, not better. (7) AUW is tight: honest all-up mass is ~1.45 kg once tilt gear and wiring are counted, not 1.3 kg.

mechanism — Induced drag CDi = CL^2/(pi*e*AR). At baseline AR~7.3, CL~0.42, raising AR to 10-12 (longer span at same area, or reduced chord) cuts CDi materially. Because a tiltrotor carries the wing as dead weight in hover there is a real optimum: too much span raises structural mass and hover bending, but in the induced-drag-limited part of the polar the gain is direct, and it grows once CD0 is reduced.

print it — Print the wing in spanwise segments over a continuous carbon tube bonded through printed ribs; keep skin a thin shell (1.2-2 mm, 2-3 perimeters) with sparse gyroid only near root. Add printed spar caps or an embedded carbon strip for bending stiffness without solid infill. Taper the planform (tip chord ~0.6 root) to approximate elliptic loading and push e toward ~0.9. · cost: Medium: longer span needs a stiffer/heavier spar to survive cruise gusts and hover download; weight penalty scales more than linearly with span. Multi-section printing/joining adds complexity. Pairs naturally with the carbon-spar lever.

10
Surface finish + low-Re laminar-flow airfoil on the wingcruisehigh priority

CRUISE + RANGE: dropping CD0 from ~0.035 to ~0.028 lifts L/D from ~9.4 to ~11 at the same CL, a ~15-20% range/endurance gain. Modest hover benefit (lower cruise power frees battery for hover reserve). Neutral on transition.

mechanism — At cruise the wing runs Re ~150k-350k where CD0 (skin-friction + pressure drag) is ~3-4x induced drag at CL~0.42. FDM layer lines (0.1-0.2 mm ridges) act as distributed roughness that trips the boundary layer early and can spawn a laminar separation bubble that spikes profile drag. Sealing/smoothing plus a section designed for this Re band (SD7037, E387, S9000-class) keeps flow attached and shrinks the bubble, cutting profile drag ~15-30%.

print it — Print with span axis vertical or use ironing on top surfaces to minimize layer-line roughness in the flow direction; keep seams off the LE suction peak. Model as a thin shell, prime + wet-sand the leading 40% of chord. Add a trip strip at 30-40% chord ONLY if flight data shows a persistent bubble. Use a validated low-Re coordinate set, not a scaled full-size section. · cost: Low money, low weight. Cost is labor: filler-primer, wet-sand to ~400-600 grit, optionally a thin heat-shrink/glass skin (~30-80 g on a 1.6 m wing). Airfoil swap is free at design time, costly to retrofit.

11
Battery chemistry, voltage, and capacity matched to the phase power profileallhigh priority

RANGE dominant (usable range can swing 20-40% via Wh/kg alone), plus a few percent system efficiency from higher voltage. Wrong chemistry either can't supply hover current (need LiPo) or wastes range (LiPo where Li-ion would do). Touches hover, transition, cruise and range together.

mechanism — Range scales with usable Wh/kg. LiPo gives high C for the brief hover/transition surge but ~180-200 Wh/kg; 21700 high-drain Li-ion gives ~250-280 Wh/kg with limited continuous C. A tiltrotor spends seconds at high power and minutes-hours cruising, so a Li-ion or Li-ion-dominant pack is often range-optimal, sized so hover surge current (I=P_hover/V) stays inside the C rating. Higher pack voltage lowers current for the same power, cutting I^2R losses in wiring/connectors/ESC and allowing thinner lighter wire. Do not oversize: dead battery mass costs range roughly linearly.

print it — Print a sliding battery tray with detents to trim CG in flight test (hover-vs-cruise CG shift is a real tiltrotor issue). Print vented 21700 holders with a snug non-compressive fit if going Li-ion. Recess an XT60/XT90 with strain relief and route short fat power leads straight to the ESCs to minimize resistance. · cost: Money (high-drain Li-ion and higher-voltage ESCs cost more) and packaging; higher voltage needs matching motor/ESC ratings; under-sized Li-ion risks sag during the hover surge.

12
Smooth blade surfaces and sharpen LE/TE (recover print-vs-molded prop loss)allmedium priority

HOVER: +3-6% thrust/power recovery (a couple points of FoM), more on a rough print. Equal benefit to CRUISE prop efficiency and lower noise. Net positive across ALL phases; it is pure loss-recovery, and it also serves as a guardrail against over-estimating thrust/endurance from paper (high-Re) prop efficiencies.

mechanism — At blade Re<~150k the boundary layer is largely laminar and separation-prone; FDM layer lines act as large relative roughness that trips and thickens it, raising section Cd and cutting thrust, and a blunt printed TE (nozzle-limited ~0.4 mm) adds base drag and noise. Low-Re prop studies show a smoothed 3D-printed rotor can match a commercial rotor's aero and acoustics. This is the concrete execution of the Reynolds-aware prop guardrail: pick diameter/RPM for the highest practical blade Re at Mtip<~0.4, then finish the blade.

print it — Print aero surfaces at <=0.12 mm layers with lines running spanwise, wet-sand 400->800 grit, finish with filler-primer (acetone-vapor for ABS/ASA). Hand-thin the TE to <0.5 mm and round the LE to the design radius. Prefer bought carbon/nylon props for the primary discs and print only the hub adapter; if printing, balance every prop. Payoff is largest on the smallest/slowest-tip rotors. · cost: Labor, not weight or money: sanding/vapor-smoothing time. Small risk of altering the airfoil if over-sanded. Vapor smoothing needs ABS/ASA (acetone) rather than PETG/PLA. Guardrail portion is free analysis discipline.

13
Thin-wall aft structure, tips, and fairings as sacrificial low-inertia masshovermedium priority

WEIGHT FRACTION + hover/transition control. Typically 40-100 g off the wing, concentrated at tips/TE so it cuts roll and yaw inertia disproportionately for snappier, less power-hungry attitude control in hover and cleaner transition.

mechanism — Aft of the spar the structure carries little bending load, but naive prints put 2-3 solid perimeters everywhere, adding mass far from CG and at the tips where it inflates roll/yaw inertia. At cruise chord Re~2.7e5 the airfoil is forgiving, so surfaces aft of the spar only need to hold shape, not carry load: make them single-perimeter skin over sparse ribs or a printed lattice.

print it — Slice everything aft of the spar and the outboard tips as 1-perimeter LW-PLA over ribs at ~30-40 mm pitch, or a printed lattice under a 0.5-0.6 mm skin. Keep the LE (impact + laminar-flow-critical) at 2 smooth perimeters. Make the tip a removable/replaceable printed part so crash damage is a cheap reprint. Consolidating each nacelle into fewer monolithic prints with press-fit bearing seats is a related nearly-free 60-160 g win in the same spirit. · cost: Weight negative, money none, complexity low. Trade: thin skins are fragile to handling, accepted as sacrificial/repairable. Below chord Re~1e5 (tiny tips) keep skins smooth and avoid ultra-rough sparse lattices so laminar separation doesn't dominate.

principles still on the bench
proposed · 01

Adjustable trailing-edge trim tabs / bendable control surfaces (the 'bend the flaps up' lesson) — decoupling built-in geometry from in-field pitch trim

The single most universal thing everyone does with a paper airplane is bend the back edge of the wing up or down to trim it — turning a nose-diving or stalling glider into a stable one without changing the airframe. None of the ten concepts embody field-adjustable/deformable trim: the powered craft rely on gyro/servo authority and the passive gliders (Polyhedra P1, and the glide modes of Albatross/Suzanne) bake pitch trim into fixed printed geometry. A 3D-printed drone concept built around compliant/bendable printed trailing edges or discrete trim tabs (leveraging TPU living hinges already used in Nakamura Locke) directly teaches that small trailing-edge deflection sets equilibrium AoA — the core paper-plane trimming principle — and de-risks the print-once-fly-once problem where a mis-estimated CG makes a fixed-geometry glider unflyable.

proposed · 02

Wing camber and leading-edge shaping / airfoil from a flat plate (folded-nose leading edge, cambered wing) — the transition from zero-thickness plate to a real lifting section

Paper airplanes teach a subtle but foundational aerodynamic lesson: a flat sheet flies poorly, but adding camber (the classic curved-wing 'glider' fold) or a thick folded leading edge dramatically improves lift and stall behavior at low Reynolds number. The entire concept set treats wings as planform/aspect-ratio problems (high-AR booms, box wings, plank) and as thrust-vectoring problems, but none is organized around exploiting the airfoil section itself — precisely the degree of freedom 3D printing unlocks that folded paper cannot (true 3D cambered/reflexed sections, tubercles, wing washout printed directly in). A concept centered on a printed cambered/reflexed airfoil (reflex being what actually makes a tailless plank like Polyhedra P1 trim without a tail) rounds out 'shape of the lifting surface,' which is currently a blind spot.

proposed · 03

Flat-spin / deep-stall recovery and spin-resistant planform geometry (the 'nose-heavy plane that pancakes vs. the one that spins in') — graceful failure and stall departure behavior

A recurring paper-flight lesson is departure behavior at the edge of the envelope: too little nose weight and the plane stalls, pitches up, and falls into a flat spin; the right CG and wing sweep/washout give a gentle mushing descent instead. The concept set covers controlled slow flight (DEP-1 blown-wing transition corridor) and intentional autorotation (Samara, Suzanne feathering), but no concept is designed around passive spin resistance / benign stall departure — geometric washout, wingtip stall delay, or CG-band sizing so the craft mushes rather than tumbles when it runs out of speed or loses power. This is the highest-consequence failure mode for the tailless/flying-wing and quadplane-in-transition designs here, and it is exactly the safety-through-geometry principle paper flight demonstrates most viscerally.

method

Each concept was designed by a dedicated engineering agent grounded in the nine verified paper designs, then adversarially reviewed by a second agent for Reynolds-number scaling, static-margin/CG soundness, printability, and quantitative honesty. That review rewrote real errors — e.g. correcting static margin to (neutral-point − CG)/MAC for tailed layouts, flagging that a TPU living hinge cannot be co-printed into a PLA/PETG surface on a single nozzle, and downgrading hand-wavy efficiency percentages to defensible ranges. Every diagram is generated from the reviewed geometry.
